CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE
The Weight of Borrowed Light

The lantern had been her grandmother's, and it burned oil the colour of weak tea. Ilse held it at arm's length anyway, because the tunnel ahead did not want to be looked at directly, and she had learned to respect that in a place.

Somewhere below, the pumps were still running. Nobody had fed them in eleven years. She stopped counting the steps at four hundred and let the sound do the counting for her — one wet thud, then a pause long enough to doubt the next.

"You could turn back," said the wall, in her brother's voice, which was the wall's favourite trick. Ilse did not answer it. Answering was how the others had lost their names.

She set the lantern down on the last dry stone, unhooked the glass, and let the flame stand naked in the draught. It leaned east. That was all she needed.
